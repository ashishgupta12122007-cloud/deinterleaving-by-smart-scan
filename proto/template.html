How it works

A model of your receiver

Positions, beam rotation, transmit power, distance and antenna pattern are rebuilt from the metadata in each file. The model reproduces which radars the fixed sweep actually heard: rank correlation , and it predicts detections where the real data holds (mean of five files).

What it learns

For every radar in the training scenarios: the chance of 20 (or 100) pulses against seconds of dwell. An isotonic regression calibrates those chances against real pulse counts, so weak long-range radars are not over-promised.

The schedule

A greedy planner spends each next 0.1 s where it adds the most expected radars, then interleaves the visits so no band waits long. It drops windows with nothing in them and stays longer where fast, weak radars are hard to catch.

Identification

DBSCAN on bearing and pulse width, then a merge of fragments that share bearing and carrier, recovers of the radars in the 60 hardest dwells per file. A random forest names the type from frequency, pulse width and PRI: top-1, top-3 on files it never saw.

Read the numbers with care. The SmartScan-ML counts come from the digital twin, not from a replay of the organizers' generator. Each scenario's schedule was learned from the other four files only. The fixed-sweep curve is measured from your pulses; its twin version is drawn dashed so you can see how close the twin is.

Your files list a 500 MHz bandwidth, but 99.985% of pulses land within 500 MHz either side of the dwell centre, so the twin uses a 1 GHz window. If the official scorer uses 250 MHz per side, re-run the planner with that value. Three to six radars per scenario transmit above 18 GHz (35 to 94 GHz); no schedule can hear them.

The waveform is synthesized from pulse descriptors (frequency, width, amplitude, arrival time). The files contain no raw IQ samples. Amplitudes in the zoomed waveform are compressed to half their dB range so weak radars stay visible; the FFT uses true levels.
